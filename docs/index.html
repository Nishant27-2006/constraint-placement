Where Does a Physical Constraint Belong in a Generative Model?

A Codimension-Controlled Benchmark on Real Power-Grid Data

Anonymous Authors
Paper under double-blind review
Under review at ICLR 2026

Abstract

Generative models are increasingly used to produce operational scenarios — plausible futures a system operator can plan against. Such scenarios must obey the physical laws of the system they describe: a set of power injections that violates Kirchhoff's laws is not a pessimistic forecast, it is an impossible one. Several methods achieve exact constraint satisfaction, and the literature disagrees about which to use. The question is not whether to enforce a constraint but where it belongs: in the hypothesis class, at inference time, in the loss, or in the coordinates.

We build a benchmark that isolates one variable — the codimension of the constraint set, the fraction of the state the physics already determines — and compare all four routes under identical backbones, budgets and data across 16 methods, 10 seeds and three suites built from real grid measurements. The answer is not universal. On a controlled contrast where the only change is how much of the state is determined, train-time projection moves from statistically indistinguishable from doing nothing (-0.07%) to a significant win (-1.96%, t = -6.29); on a third real dataset the same code is significantly worse (+3.71%, t = +5.50).

Two further results cut against the field's habits. Soft penalties — the default in physics-informed generative modelling — are dominated on both axes by every exact route: raising the penalty weight three orders of magnitude leaves the violation at the same order while destroying the score. And in a downstream stochastic unit-commitment study, scenario feasibility does not reach the scheduling decision at all: cost regret is essentially uncorrelated with the energy score and strongly correlated with calibration. All claims were pre-registered with explicit falsification conditions before the sweep ran; two of our own predictions were falsified by our own data, and we report them as falsified.

Takeaway. The effect is not a constant that a better implementation would move — it changes sign, significantly in both directions, on the same code. The clean causal claim is the grid-noflow → grid contrast. The third suite is a genuinely different data-generating process (accounting identities rather than Kirchhoff), so it is a confounded point, but it establishes that real problems exist where exact projection costs fidelity. Channel scale heterogeneity was checked and rejected as an alternative explanation.

Anyone reporting a single winner has measured one suite.

Soft Penalties Are Dominated on Both Axes

Feasibility Is Not Decision Value

Proper scoring rules are the currency of this literature. We also ran the decision they stand in for: two-stage stochastic unit commitment, commitment frozen on the generated scenarios, scored on the realised day, regret measured against perfect foresight.

Takeaway, and the result we least expected. Physical feasibility of the scenario set does not reach the scheduling decision at all; calibration carries it. The exactly-feasible train-time routes take the worst regret among neural methods (FM+reduced 66%, FM+DC3 58%, ours 53%) and the best belong to the two most over-dispersed baselines (GaussianCopula 16%, NormFlow-RealNVP 16%). Exactly-feasible ensembles are sharper, the scheduler trusts them, under-commits reserve and pays in load shed.

A study that stopped at the energy score would have reported the opposite conclusion with confidence. Stated with its limit: this stage has 3 seeds, not 10.

Takeaway. Removing the determined directions from the hypothesis class buys far more than any solver schedule, because an adaptive solver spends its budget integrating directions that are known in closed form. The violation sits at 0.000534 MW against 457 MW. Efficiency is reported as NFE and analytic FLOPs — exactly countable and hardware-independent — not as device joules.

What We Predicted, and Got Wrong

Every claim was written into a pre-registration with an explicit falsification condition before the sweep ran, so the acceptance criteria could not drift to fit the output. Two conditions fired.

Falsified · C3

We predicted train-time ≤ inference-time ≤ post-hoc on fidelity, universally.

Measured: the ordering holds on grid (-3.39% for train-time against inference-time, t = -9.09) and reverses on measured (+3.38%, t = +5.05). The reversal became the paper's central finding.

Falsified · C5′

We predicted that under an N-1 topology swap, routes working in physical coordinates keep their learned distribution while chart-based routes do not.

Measured: ours 896.4 against FM+reduced 881.8 — indistinguishable — and both beaten by FM+posthoc 651.5. Per the pre-registration the transfer differentiator was dropped from the paper rather than softened.

Negative result · AC manifold

We predicted that per-step retraction would be needed to bound drift on the nonlinear AC power-flow manifold.

Measured: a single retraction after the solve reaches |g|∞ = 0.0077 from 5.1, essentially free in score. Per-step retraction at a budget of 3 Gauss-Newton iterations reaches only 129 — worse than doing nothing — because the in-loop pull-back never converges and injects a biased correction at every step. Reported with the diagnosed cause, not omitted.

A non-neural baseline places second of sixteen on both grid suites. kNN-Historical resamples analogue days: zero parameters, zero function evaluations, exactly feasible by construction. It beats every GAN, VAE and normalising flow on all three suites. The pre-registration committed us to putting it in the abstract if it won, so it is in the abstract.
